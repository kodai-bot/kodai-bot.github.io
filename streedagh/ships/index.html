Streedagh · 1588 · a ship rebuilt from numbers

Standing room

La Juliana was one of the three Armada ships that broke up at Streedagh. She carried about 65–70 mariners and 290–325 soldiers. No drawing of her survives, and the wreck lies in pieces on the seabed. What does survive is a column of administrative figures: her tonnage rating, and the measured dimensions of ships of her squadron.

From those alone you can ask a plain question: where did three hundred soldiers stand? The answer forces a shape on the ship. A single deck at each end is nowhere near enough. Stepped castles, two and three decks high, fore and aft, come close.

Her sisters, to scale

Four ships whose dimensions were recorded in codos, drawn in plan at the same scale. Juliana's own dimensions are lost, but her rating is known (860 wartime toneladas, so 716.7 machos). Fitting her rating against the three broad-bodied ships gives her size three ways. The duke's galleon is long and narrow, a different family, so it is left out of the fit.

A ruler that doesn't add up. The source gives the codo as 33/48 of a vara of 835.9 mm, and prints the result as 547.7 mm. The arithmetic gives 574.7 mm. The reconstruction uses the arithmetic and flags the printed figure as a probable slip. Using 547.7 would shrink every ship here by 4.7%, and every deck area by nearly twice that.

What it says, and what it doesn't

One deck at each end can't do it

With single-level castles, even the most generous proportions give about 1.0 m² per soldier, and the central case 0.75. Three hundred men would have had to fill the waist the mariners needed to sail the ship.

Stepped castles nearly can

In the central case, two decks forward and two and a half aft give 1.29 m² per soldier from the upperworks alone, and reach the allowance once part of the deck below is used. That is the shape contemporary pictures suggest, reached here without looking at them.

Not a floor plan

Nobody had a private 1.5 m². Soldiers shared space in shifts, used the open roofs in fair weather, and crowded below in foul. The figures say how much floor the ship must have had, not how it was lived in.

Still to verify

Which codo the clerks measured with, and the rating formula with its apparent 3% deduction, both need checking against the primary sources before these numbers can be called a reconstruction rather than a test.
