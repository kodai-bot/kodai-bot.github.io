Streedagh, the three wreck sites

Where to dig first

or rather, where doing nothing costs most

The sand that buried the Streedagh wrecks also moves. In 1985 and again in 2015 storms stripped it off Site 1 and exposed guns, wheels, a rudder and articulated hull. Uncovered timber is eaten and abraded within seasons. This page sets each site's material on the seabed, taken from the mass budget, against how often the sea uncovers it and how much of what it uncovers is lost. The result ranks the sites for monitoring, rescue and excavation. It is not a storm forecast.

Three sites, mass against exposure

The sites are numbered as in Birch & McElvogue (1999); the ship named on each is the attribution held in the site record, with its credence. Bars show the median; whiskers show the 5–95% range over 100,000 draws.

If nothing is done

The cumulative tonnage lost from each site over the coming decades if exposure goes on at the rates the record suggests. Each year the same share of what remains is lost. Shaded bands show the 5–95% range. Rates are held constant: a stormier future or a thinning sand budget would steepen every line.

Why it isn't all gone already

At this rate Site 1 loses about 2.3% of what remains each decade. If that had held since 1588, about 63% of the site would be gone by now, yet divers found articulated hull in 1985, and in 2015 a rudder came out after long burial. The rate on this page cannot have held for four centuries. The likely history comes in three phases:

1588: the first months and years

Losses were heavy. Whatever stood proud of the seabed broke up, washed onto the strand or was eaten by shipworm. What survives is mostly what the sand buried quickly.

Centuries under the sand

Buried in sand with almost no oxygen, timber and iron change very little. Over this long phase the loss rate was close to zero.

Now: uncovered again

Storms have stripped Site 1 in recent decades (1985, 2015). Only now does the rate on this page apply. That points to a change in the sand around the site, from storms, a shifting sand supply or climate, rather than steady wear.

So read the numbers this way. They are the cost of the regime the site is in now, not a rate since 1588. That most of the site has not already gone is evidence that the exposure is recent or has got worse, which is a reason to act, not to wait. The tonnes are an upper bound: they apply the loss to the mass the budget puts on the seabed on the wreck day, as if it were all still there. The percentage, a share of whatever actually remains, is the sounder figure.

What goes first

The share of each kind of material lost per decade, median with its range. Timber and organics go first; bronze lasts longest. The divers recorded that uncovered organics are "attacked by flora and fauna" and that sand abrades the bronze guns. How fast each class is lost is a stated assumption hypothesis, the same at every site.

What decides the ranking

How strongly each assumption moves Site 1's lead over the next site (rank correlation over the draws). Two priors dominate: how often Site 1 is uncovered, and how much of a site one storm uncovers. Both could be measured with repeated sand-level surveys.

What is new here, and what is not. That Site 1 comes first mostly restates the record: its exposure rate was set from the same accounts that call it "the most dynamic". The model adds the scale, in tonnes a decade with an interval, and it names the assumption that matters most. It also finds that Site 3 holds almost as much material as Site 1 while losing the least.

The record it rests on

Every exposure rate on this page comes from these descriptions. The record is thin, which is why the rates are wide. One dated exposure at Site 2 or Site 3 would change the ranking more than any refinement of the model.

    What would sharpen it

    Dated sand levels at each site

    Repeated bathymetry or diver sand-level readings would turn the exposure priors into measured rates. If the National Monuments Service monitoring were released, it would settle the top driver directly.

    The recent surveys' positions

    Fixing the 2015 finds and the later surveys to positions would move the within-site targets from words to a map.

    An exposure at Site 2

    Site 2 is a compact, dense spread known only from isolated finds. One recorded exposure there would change its rank more than anything else.
