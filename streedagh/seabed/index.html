Streedagh, 25 September 1588

What went down

and what it would take

Three Armada ships broke up off Streedagh strand in an hour. How much of them reached the seabed? Not from their wartime tonnage, which was a hire rating, not a weight, but built up part by part: the hulls from the ships' own measurements, the guns and powder from the muster, the ballast from an excavated sister wreck, the stores from the fleet's ration scale. Each part says where it comes from and how it is known.

Where the mass went

Each bar is one part of the load on the wreck day. The filled part stayed on the seabed; the dashed remainder broke away and drove onto the strand, as Cuéllar saw it, "the strand covered with timber". The whisker is the 5–95% range of the seabed share. How much of each part stayed is itself an assumption hypothesis, and the largest one.

Medians of the parts do not add up exactly to the median of the whole: every part is drawn together, 100,000 times, and each figure is read off its own distribution. The people aboard are not counted as mass anywhere: the site is a war grave.

What decides the answer

Which assumption moves the seabed total most (rank correlation over the draws). The top three each have a field test: how much buried hull lies under the ballast, how heavy the hull was, and the ballast mounds themselves, measured as Martin measured the Rosa's.

If a fraction survives

What a museum and conservation facility would have to hold and treat if this share of the seabed material survives and is raised. A planning bracket, not an estimate: the fraction is applied to every kind of material alike, and every figure carries the uncertainty of the budget beneath it. Timber treatment anchored on the Vasa (17 years of PEG), the Mary Rose (about 19) and the Bremen cog (about 18–20, in an 800 m³ tank); iron on the Queen Anne's Revenge lab (about four years per cannon).

Predictions waiting for data

Every figure above is also a prediction in the site record, stated before the evidence that would test it, with what would prove it wrong. Surveys of Streedagh have been made since the 1980s and few are published; each of these says exactly what such data would settle.
